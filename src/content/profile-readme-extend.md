## How the question changed

The question behind my research is: **how could I build a system that learns to understand an unfamiliar world?** I began by asking how intelligence could live in fixed numerical weights. Deriving learning and optimization by hand showed me how weights change, but not how a system comes to understand its surroundings. I suspected that the training rule or the hardware itself might matter. I needed to test those intuitions.

[ChronoForge](/repositories/chronoforge-fpga-engine), a game engine running directly in FPGA logic, made the hardware question concrete. In [Bio-Analog CPU](/repositories/bio-analog-cpu), I tried to design a learner with resident weights and local updates. My first rule looked promising until I worked through the mathematics: knowing how much a weight contributed did not tell it which *direction* to move, and the rule did not escape the computation I had hoped to avoid. I abandoned it and rebuilt the system as a forward-only learner with a small classifier. Simulations showed where it retained information across changing data and where tuned baselines did better. I had found **a way to train a learner**, but still not an answer to how it understands a new world.

That distinction sent me back to modern ML, which I had been too quick to dismiss. Building [my-harness](/repositories/my-harness) showed me how much useful agent behavior comes from the tools and system around a fixed model. I then studied transformers and memory to ask: could an agent use earlier observations to choose what to inspect next? In [Active Sensing](/repositories/active-sensing-vis), a visual model learned to point at useful locations. Yet a state borrowed from another image worked about as well as its own, and a policy blind to its previous glimpses pointed better. The controls did not support the kind of memory I was looking for.

After months of testing other explanations, I closed that particular setup. A credible next test required a major redesign, with no evidence yet that it would reveal the mechanism I wanted. This was a **scoped negative result** about my experiment, not a proof that active sensing cannot work. It changed how I work: I define what would count as learning and what could disprove my interpretation before trusting a good score. It also moved my question from where learning happens to **what an agent does to learn from experience**.

## What I'm working on now

After that result, I asked how I had learned enough to diagnose an unfamiliar problem myself. I chose what to read and test, then changed my view when the evidence disagreed. That experience motivates my current question: can an agent learn a *policy for learning*—a way to choose informative actions and revise its understanding as it goes? It is an analogy that motivates the work, not a claim that I have reproduced human learning.

In [Causal World Learning and Transfer](/repositories/causal-world-learning-and-transfer), I made that question small enough to test. An agent acts in an unfamiliar Boolean world and observes what changes. Exact inference shows which hidden rules remain possible; random and information-gain policies provide baselines. This lets me measure what the evidence permits before asking a learned policy to act under a limited budget. Eventually, I want to study what one agent can pass to another through a limited channel. The world, inference, and baselines are current work; the learned policy and transfer remain open.
